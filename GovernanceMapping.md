# Multilateral Governance Mapping for Global Catastrophic and Existential Risks

**Project:** Global Risk Governance Monitor
**Deliverable 3 of 4:** For each of the 16 risks in `MasterList.md`, an inventory of the multilateral and global governance mechanisms that exist to assess and/or manage it, an assessment of how adequate each is, and an identification of the governance gaps.
**Date:** 4 September 2026 (v2 — aligned to the 16-risk master list; UN Global Risk Report 2024 added as source)
**Companion file:** `MasterList.md` (risk definitions, taxonomy, likelihood/impact, interconnections). Risk codes (N1–U1) are shared across both files and the dashboard.

## Part A — Methodology

### A.1 What counts as a "multilateral or global governance mechanism"

For this mapping, a mechanism is included if it meets **both**:

1. **Membership is multilateral or global** — it is constituted by three or more states, or by a universal/near-universal international body, or it operates under a global intergovernmental mandate. Purely national instruments (e.g., the US Planetary Defense Coordination Office, or a national AI act) are noted only as context, not counted as global governance. Regional bodies (e.g., the EU, the Council of Europe) are included **only** where they are explicitly designed to be open to, or set a de facto standard for, the wider world.
2. **It performs a governance function on the risk** — it assesses, monitors, regulates, coordinates, finances, or responds to the risk. This spans the full spectrum from binding treaties to standing UN agencies to scientific assessment panels to soft-law declarations and coordination networks.

Private-sector self-governance, industry standards, and NGO advocacy are outside the definition but are flagged where they fill a vacuum left by the absence of public mechanisms (this itself is a finding).

### A.2 The five assessment dimensions

Each risk's governance is assessed against five dimensions. Together they answer not just "does something exist?" but "is it the *right* something, and does it *work*?"

**1. Function — assess vs. manage.** Does the mechanism only *study, monitor, and warn* about the risk (assessment), or does it also *reduce, regulate, or respond to* it (management)? This distinction matters enormously: the world is consistently better at assessing risks than at managing them — the UN Global Risk Report 2024 (UN24) finds risk *identification* to be the multilateral system's greatest perceived strength and *response* its weakness. Coded as **Assess / Manage / Both**.

**2. Bindingness.** How enforceable is the mechanism? A four-step ladder:
- **Binding + verified** — a treaty with an independent verification/compliance body and consequences (strongest).
- **Binding** — a legally binding treaty, but without real verification or enforcement.
- **Soft law** — political declarations, frameworks, voluntary targets, codes of conduct.
- **Advisory / none** — assessment or dialogue only, or no instrument at all.

**3. Inclusiveness.** Are the actors who matter actually inside the mechanism? A regime can be near-universal yet miss the two or three states that determine the risk. Coded as **Universal / Broad / Club / Narrow**, with a note on which critical actors are absent.

**4. Scope adequacy.** Does the mechanism's mandate cover the whole risk, or only a slice of it?

**5. Effectiveness.** Is there evidence the mechanism is actually holding the risk down — a track record, measurable results, or at least credible capability? Legal form is not a proxy for this. Coded qualitatively as **Proven / Partial / Weak / Untested**.

### A.3 Overall coverage rating

Synthesising the five dimensions, each risk is given a single **governance-coverage rating** for quick comparison and for the dashboard:

- **Robust** — a binding, broadly inclusive, well-scoped regime with a track record (rare).
- **Developing** — real institutions and instruments exist and function, but with material weaknesses in bindingness, scope, or effectiveness.
- **Weak** — fragmented, mostly soft-law, or ineffective coverage; large parts of the risk ungoverned.
- **Minimal** — little or no dedicated multilateral governance; effectively an orphan risk.

A rating may carry a trend flag (**↓ eroding**, **↑ strengthening**) where the trajectory is significant. Where UN24's survey rated the risk as a "Global Vulnerability" (high importance, low preparedness), this is noted as independent corroboration.

### A.4 The benchmark: a solved risk

One risk is deliberately used as a yardstick without appearing on the live list. **Stratospheric ozone depletion** was a genuine global catastrophic threat in the 1980s and was governed to near-solution by the **Vienna Convention (1985)**, the **Montreal Protocol (1987)** — the only universally ratified treaty in UN history — and the **Kigali Amendment (2016)**, backed by the **Multilateral Fund** and standing scientific panels. The ozone layer is on track to recover by mid-century. The regime combined five ingredients: a binding treaty, independent verification, universal membership, financing for the transition, and adjustability to tighten controls over time. It is the reference point for the word "Robust" throughout this document.

## Part B — Per-risk governance mapping

### Family N — Natural risks

**N1. Asteroid / comet impact — Coverage: Developing**
*Mechanisms:* UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) endorsed, and the UN General Assembly established (2013), two standing bodies: the **International Asteroid Warning Network (IAWN)**, chaired by NASA, which coordinates detection, tracking and characterisation (assessment), and the **Space Mission Planning Advisory Group (SMPAG)**, chaired by ESA, which does mitigation/deflection planning. Deflection is technically proven (NASA's DART, 2022). National assets do the heavy lifting.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both, weighted to assessment. **Bindingness** Advisory — IAWN/SMPAG recommendations are not enforceable. **Inclusiveness** Broad but incomplete. **Scope** Strong on detection/warning; thin on long-period comets and on the *decision* to act. **Effectiveness** Detection proven and exercised — the 2024 YR4 scare activated IAWN/SMPAG procedures for the first time in earnest. UN24 flags "space-based events" as an overlooked, under-prepared risk.
*Gap:* **No binding protocol for who authorises, commands, and funds an actual deflection mission.** Detection is governed; the response decision is not.

**N2. Supervolcanic eruption — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* No dedicated global regime. The **UNDRR Sendai Framework** covers disaster risk reduction generically; scientific coordination runs through non-governmental bodies (IAVCEI, Global Volcano Model); ICAO's Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres address aviation ash only; WHO/OCHA would handle humanitarian response.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess at best. **Bindingness** None. **Scope** Grossly inadequate — nothing addresses the defining threat of multi-year global agricultural collapse. **Effectiveness** Untested.
*Gap:* **No mechanism monitors supervolcanoes as a global catastrophic threat, and none prepares for global harvest failure** (strategic food reserves, resilient-food contingency planning). Unpreventable and near-ungoverned.

**N3. Severe space weather — Coverage: Weak**
*Mechanisms:* The **World Meteorological Organization** coordinates space-weather services; **ICAO** has run global space-weather advisory centres for aviation since 2019; the **International Space Environment Service (ISES)** links national forecasting centres; COPUOS and UNDRR provide fora.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess (monitoring/forecasting); management is national grid-hardening. **Bindingness** None globally. **Scope** Covers forecasting, not resilience standards for the systems at risk. **Effectiveness** Partial — good short-term forecasting, little mitigation. UN24: an under-prepared "space-based" risk.
*Gap:* **No binding global standards for hardening electricity grids and critical infrastructure** against a Carrington-scale event.

**N4. Natural pandemic — Coverage: Developing ↑**
*Mechanisms:* The **World Health Organization**; the **International Health Regulations (2005)**, legally binding on 196 states (2024 amendments strengthen them); the **WHO Pandemic Agreement**, adopted 20 May 2025 — only WHO's second-ever binding treaty — but **not yet in force**: its Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) annex is still under negotiation, with adoption targeted for May 2027. Supporting bodies: **CEPI**, **Gavi**, the **WHO Hub for Pandemic and Epidemic Intelligence**.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both. **Bindingness** Binding (IHR) but historically weakly enforced; the new Agreement adds binding architecture once ratified. **Inclusiveness** Near-universal — but the 2025 US departure from WHO is a serious hole. **Scope** Broad, though equity and supply-sharing remain unresolved. **Effectiveness** Partial — COVID-19 exposed compliance, speed and equity failures. UN24 places pandemics in its under-prepared "societal cluster."
*Gap:* **IHR compliance and enforcement, the unfinished equity/PABS bargain, sustained financing, and the withdrawal of a major power.**

### Family T — Anthropogenic-technological risks

**T1. Nuclear war (including AI-compressed escalation) — Coverage: Weak ↓ (eroding)**
*Mechanisms:* The **Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT, 1970)** anchors the regime; the **IAEA** verifies safeguards; the **CTBT** is not in force but its CTBTO runs a global monitoring network; the **TPNW (2021)** is in force but no nuclear-armed state has joined; regional nuclear-weapon-free-zone treaties cover much of the global South. **Bilateral US–Russia arms control has collapsed: New START expired on 5 February 2026 with no successor and no negotiations underway** — the first time since 1972 that no treaty limits the two largest arsenals. The Conference on Disarmament remains deadlocked. On the *AI-escalation* dimension: the **CCW Group of Governmental Experts on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems** has deliberated since 2014 without an instrument; the **REAIM** process (2023, 2024) produced a political declaration on responsible military AI; a 2023 UNGA resolution drew 156 states. Support for negotiating a binding LAWS instrument reached 70+ states in March 2026 and could be mandated at the **CCW Review Conference in November 2026** — but the US and Russia oppose binding rules, and **nothing at all governs AI integration into nuclear command and control.**
*Assessment:* **Function** Both. **Bindingness** Binding + verified for non-proliferation; **the binding cap on deployed strategic arsenals has lapsed**; autonomous-weapons rules are soft law only. **Inclusiveness** NPT broad, but the arms-control layer excludes the actors that matter — China is in no framework, and the nine possessors sit outside the TPNW. **Scope** Non-proliferation well-covered; arms limitation and the AI–nuclear nexus uncovered. **Effectiveness** Partial and worsening.
*Gap:* **No binding numerical limits on the largest arsenals; China outside any arms-control regime; no rules on autonomous weapons or human control; the AI–nuclear escalation nexus entirely ungoverned.** A regime moving from Developing toward Weak in real time.

**T2. Anthropogenic pandemic (engineered or accidental; AI-facilitated) — Coverage: Weak**
*Mechanisms:* The **Biological Weapons Convention (BWC, 1975)** bans bioweapons and binds 180+ states — but has **no verification organisation or compliance mechanism**, a gap unclosed for 50 years. The **UN Secretary-General's Mechanism** can investigate alleged use. The **Australia Group** coordinates export controls (a club of ~40 states). WHO/IHR and the new Pandemic Agreement touch biosafety and lab-leak questions. On the *AI-enabler* dimension, the only relevant instruments are voluntary frontier-lab commitments and the nascent UN AI bodies (see T3).
*Assessment:* **Function** Manage (prohibition) but not verify. **Bindingness** Binding but unverifiable — the defining weakness. **Inclusiveness** BWC broad; Australia Group exclusionary. **Scope** Bans state bioweapons; leaves **dual-use research, lab biosafety, and AI-enabled pathogen design ungoverned.** **Effectiveness** Weak — no way to detect cheating. UN24: "biorisks" sit in the under-prepared societal cluster.
*Gap:* **No BWC verification protocol; no global governance of dual-use biotechnology or of the AI–bio convergence** that is collapsing the expertise barrier to engineered pathogens.

**T3. Loss of human control over advanced AI — Coverage: Minimal → Weak ↑**
*Mechanisms:* **No binding global treaty on AI safety.** What exists is a fast-emerging patchwork: the **Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (2024)** — the first binding international AI treaty, signed by the US, UK and EU, but focused on human rights rather than loss-of-control; the **UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** (established August 2025, appointed February 2026) — a nascent "IPCC for AI" (assessment); the **Global Dialogue on AI Governance** (first convened Geneva, July 2026); the **Global Digital Compact** (2024); the **OECD AI Principles** and **UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics** (soft law); and the summit / AI-Safety-Institute network (Bletchley → Seoul → Paris). The EU AI Act is regional.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assessment and dialogue are forming; management is voluntary. **Bindingness** Almost none for safety. **Inclusiveness** The UN bodies are universal in principle, but capability is concentrated: ~75% of frontier training compute in the US, ~15% in China. **Scope** Nothing governs frontier-model development, compute, or loss-of-control risk. **Effectiveness** Untested; the UN panel's own July 2026 finding is that **capabilities are accelerating faster than any government can regulate**, with "no technical guarantee" that advanced systems follow instructions. UN24: AI and frontier technologies form an under-prepared "technological cluster" where "institutions struggle to catch up."
*Gap:* The **single largest gap relative to risk magnitude** — no binding safety regime, no frontier-model oversight, no governance of compute. Institutions are appearing (hence ↑) but far behind the technology.

**T4. Other WMD (chemical, radiological) — Coverage: Robust (chemical) / Developing (radiological)**
*Mechanisms:* The **Chemical Weapons Convention (1997)** with the **OPCW** is the gold standard alongside ozone: near-universal, legally binding, with intrusive verification and destruction of declared stockpiles (Nobel Peace Prize 2013). Radiological security runs through the **IAEA**, the **Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material** (and 2005 amendment), the **International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism**, and the IAEA Code of Conduct on radioactive sources.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both (chemical: verify + manage). **Bindingness** Binding + verified (chemical); binding (radiological). **Inclusiveness** Near-universal for CWC. **Scope** Chemical well-covered; radiological source-security uneven. **Effectiveness** Proven for chemical — though Syria and targeted assassinations show enforcement limits when a state defies the regime.
*Gap:* **Uneven radiological-source security, and enforcement against a defiant state.** The chemical regime is otherwise the model the others could follow.

### Family E — Anthropogenic-environmental risks

**E1. Catastrophic climate change — Coverage: Developing (comprehensive but under-performing)**
*Mechanisms:* The **UNFCCC (1992)**, the **Paris Agreement (2015)**, and the **IPCC** form the most complete architecture of any risk here — universal membership, annual COPs, and finance vehicles (Green Climate Fund, loss-and-damage fund). But Paris targets (NDCs) are **self-set and not legally binding**; only the *procedures* bind.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both — exemplary assessment, managing via Paris. **Bindingness** Procedurally binding, substantively voluntary. **Inclusiveness** Near-universal, though repeated US withdrawals undermine it. **Scope** Comprehensive. **Effectiveness** **Weak-to-partial — the defining problem:** emissions and warming track well above the 1.5 °C pathway. UN24 ranks "climate change inaction" the top risk worldwide and part of the under-prepared environmental cluster — "some preparedness exists, but insufficient relative to the scale."
*Gap:* **No enforcement of national targets, a persistent ambition-and-finance shortfall, no mechanism to compel the largest emitters.** An effectiveness gap, not an architecture gap.

**E2. Ecological collapse, biodiversity loss & resource depletion — Coverage: Weak–Developing**
*Mechanisms:* The **Convention on Biological Diversity (1993)**; the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022)** with 23 targets for 2030 ("30×30"); **IPBES** (assessment); sectoral treaties — **CITES**, **Ramsar**, **CMS**; the **BBNJ / High Seas Treaty**, in force since 17 January 2026, closing a long-standing gap over half the planet. Pollution and resources: the **Minamata Convention** (mercury), **Basel/Rotterdam/Stockholm** conventions (waste and chemicals), the **UNCCD** (land), and a global plastics treaty still under negotiation. COP16 (2024–25) agreed a US$200 billion/year finance strategy.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both, assessment stronger. **Bindingness** Binding frameworks, **voluntary targets.** **Inclusiveness** Broad — but the **United States is not a party to the CBD.** **Scope** Broad on paper; freshwater and soil largely ungoverned globally. **Effectiveness** Weak — the predecessor Aichi targets were almost entirely missed; finance far behind. UN24 rates pollution the second most important global risk and biodiversity decline and resource shortage as under-prepared.
*Gap:* **Enforcement, finance mobilisation, the absence of the US, and no global regime for freshwater, soil or resource scarcity.**

**E3. Earth-system tipping points (incl. geoengineering failure) — Coverage: Minimal (structural orphan)**
*Mechanisms:* **No governance regime is organised around Earth-system integrity or tipping-point interactions as such.** Individual elements are covered by separate silos — climate (UNFCCC), biodiversity (CBD), oceans (UNCLOS, BBNJ), ozone (Montreal). The *interactions* — AMOC collapse, ice-sheet loss, permafrost thaw, nutrient cycles — have **no institutional home**; "planetary boundaries" remains a scientific construct. On *geoengineering*: the CBD adopted a non-binding moratorium (2010); the London Convention/Protocol restricts ocean fertilisation; a UNEA resolution on solar radiation modification failed in 2024. Nothing governs stratospheric aerosol injection or termination shock.
*Assessment:* **Function** Fragmented assessment; no integrated management. **Bindingness** None at Earth-system level; soft moratoria on geoengineering. **Scope** The scope gap *is* the risk. UN24 lists geoengineering disasters among the longer-horizon risks (16+ years).
*Gap:* **The largest structural gap on the map.** GCF 2026's core argument is precisely for new "planetary commons" governance to fill it — and deliberate planetary intervention is proceeding faster than any rule for it.

### Family G — Geopolitical, societal and systemic risks

**G1. Great-power & state-based armed conflict — Coverage: Weak ↓ (eroding)**
*Mechanisms:* The **UN Charter** and **Security Council**; the **ICJ** and **ICC**; UN peacekeeping; regional organisations (NATO, AU, ASEAN, OSCE). Arms-control regimes (T1) overlap.
*Assessment:* **Function** Manage (collective security). **Bindingness** Charter binding, but enforcement runs through a Council **paralysed by the P5 veto.** **Inclusiveness** Universal, but the veto lets any of five states block action; major powers sit outside the ICC. **Scope** Designed for this risk; **structurally unable to act when a great power is the aggressor.** **Effectiveness** Weak and declining — Ukraine, Gaza, and the "Age of Competition" WEF 2026 describes. UN24 finds geopolitical tensions the most interconnected risk of all and "collapse of multilateral institutions" a named risk in its own right.
*Gap:* **Security Council reform is blocked; no effective check on aggression by (or protected by) a permanent member.**

**G2. Governance failure & information collapse — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* The **Global Digital Compact (2024)**; the UN **Global Principles for Information Integrity (2024)**; UNESCO's platform-governance and media-literacy work; the Human Rights Council on freedom of expression. **No binding mechanism governs disinformation, platform accountability, or the integrity of the information environment**, and nothing addresses AI-generated synthetic media at scale.
*Assessment:* **Function** Soft coordination. **Bindingness** None. **Scope** Minimal versus a transnational, privately owned information ecosystem. **Effectiveness** Weak/untested; in tension with free-expression protections. UN24 identifies mis- and disinformation as *the* stand-out Global Vulnerability — the risk rated most important and least prepared for, already occurring for 80% of respondents, and facing "unique barriers" to action.
*Gap:* **Near-total.** Because this is the *meta-risk* that degrades every other mechanism's ability to function, the gap is doubly consequential.

**G3. Global systemic & infrastructure collapse — Coverage: Weak–Developing (uneven)**
*Mechanisms:* **Financial** systemic risk is comparatively well-governed by soft-but-effective bodies — **FSB**, **IMF**, **BIS/Basel Committee**, **G20**. **Cyber** governance is nascent: the **UN Convention against Cybercrime** (adopted December 2024, opened for signature October 2025) has 70+ signatories but **only three ratifications and is not in force**; UN norms of responsible state behaviour are non-binding. Energy, food, water and supply-chain resilience are handled by fragmented sectoral bodies (IEA, FAO, WFP).
*Assessment:* **Function** Both in finance; mostly assess elsewhere. **Bindingness** Soft law (finance, effective) to nascent-binding (cyber). **Scope** No mechanism addresses **cascading, cross-sector systemic risk** as a whole. **Effectiveness** Partial (finance) to weak (cyber, cross-sector). UN24 places cybersecurity breakdown in its under-prepared technological cluster.
*Gap:* **No integrated governance of cascading systemic risk or infrastructure interdependence**; cyber governance real but not in force.

**G4. Technology-driven power concentration (AI-facilitated) — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* Almost none at global level. Competition/antitrust law is **national**. The closest instruments are tax-related: the **OECD/G20 global minimum corporate tax (15%)** — weakened by a January 2026 "side-by-side" arrangement accommodating US objections — and the **UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation**, under negotiation for adoption in 2027. **No mechanism governs concentration of transformative technology (AI compute), monopoly power, or the lock-in pathway.** The UN Scientific Panel on AI now *documents* compute concentration (assessment) but has no mandate to act on it.
*Assessment:* **Function** Partial assessment. **Bindingness** Soft/negotiating. **Inclusiveness** OECD is a club; the UN tax process is broader but early. **Scope** Addresses tax slivers, not power or technological lock-in. **Effectiveness** Weak/too early. UN24 names "technology-driven power concentration" as a distinct risk inside its under-prepared technological cluster.
*Gap:* **Among the least-governed risks** — no global antitrust, no governance of AI-power concentration, nothing aimed at the existential lock-in pathway.

### Family U — Unknown and emerging risks

**U1. Unknown / unforeseen & emerging risks — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* The **Pact for the Future (2024)** and its **Declaration on Future Generations** commit the UN to strategic foresight; UN24 itself is the first output of a new UN risk-foresight function, and its four scenarios (Breakdown → Breakthrough) are explicitly anticipatory. But institutionalised horizon-scanning for novel catastrophic risks remains nascent and non-binding.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess (foresight), aspirationally. **Bindingness** None. **Scope** By nature never complete; capacity thin. **Effectiveness** Untested — the system remains largely reactive.
*Gap:* **No standing, well-resourced anticipatory mechanism** to detect and govern risks before they mature.

## Part C — Overarching assessment

Seven cross-cutting findings emerge from the mapping. Together they describe a multilateral system that is far better at naming catastrophic risks than at governing them, and that is weakening precisely as the risks intensify.

**1. The system assesses far better than it manages.** For almost every risk, the assessment layer is stronger than the management layer. The world has authoritative scientific bodies — the IPCC, IPBES, the new UN AI panel, IAWN, CTBTO monitoring, and now UN24's own risk survey — but the instruments that would actually *reduce* the risks are weaker, softer, or missing. UN24 says this in the multilateral system's own voice: institutions are rated effective at *identifying* risk and weak at *responding*. Knowing is not governing.

**2. Genuinely robust regimes are rare, and they share a common recipe.** Only the chemical-weapons regime within T4 qualifies as **Robust** on the live list — and the benchmark that defines the word is a risk no longer on it, ozone (Part A.4). Both combine the same five ingredients: a **binding** treaty, **independent verification**, **near-universal** membership, **financing** for the transition, and **adjustability** to tighten over time. Where these co-occur, catastrophic risks have been driven down dramatically. Where any is missing — verification in the BWC, enforcement in Paris, universality in the CBD — effectiveness collapses. This is the template the dashboard should highlight.

**3. Governance maturity is inversely related to risk velocity and novelty.** The risks that are newest, fastest-moving, and rated most severe for the long term — **loss of control over AI (T3), AI-facilitated anthropogenic pandemics (T2), Earth-system tipping points (E3), and technology-driven power concentration (G4)** — are precisely the least governed. UN24 independently identifies the same "technological cluster" as the place where "institutions struggle to catch up with rapid advances." The mature regimes govern older, more discrete hazards. The frontier of risk has outrun the frontier of governance.

**4. The multilateral system is eroding at the moment it is most needed.** Several load-bearing mechanisms have weakened in 2025–2026: **New START expired (February 2026) with no successor**; the **Security Council remains veto-paralysed**; and major powers have **withdrawn from key regimes** (the US from the WHO and, repeatedly, from the Paris process). UN24 lists "collapse of multilateral institutions" as a risk in its own right and finds "weak governance and lack of consensus" to be the top barriers to acting on *every* risk. Governance is not merely lagging — in places it is going backwards.

**5. Several catastrophic risks are effectively orphans, with no institutional home.** **Supervolcanoes (N2), Earth-system tipping points and geoengineering as an interacting whole (E3), technology-driven power concentration (G4), information collapse (G2), and unknown/emerging risks (U1)** have little or no dedicated multilateral governance. These are not risks that are governed badly; they are risks that are barely governed at all.

**6. Governance is siloed while the risks are interconnected.** As `MasterList.md` §7 sets out, the risks amplify one another — AI accelerates bio-risk and disinformation; climate drives conflict and displacement; nuclear war is itself a climate catastrophe. UN24's network analysis shows the same: geopolitical tension cascades into war, WMD, displacement and rule-of-law collapse. Yet governance is organised in vertical silos with **no mechanism that addresses cascades, convergence, or polycrisis.** The AI–bio convergence, the AI–nuclear-command nexus, and the climate–conflict chain each fall between separate regimes.

**7. Legal form is not the same as effectiveness.** The Paris Agreement is binding-in-procedure yet under-performs; the BWC is binding yet unverifiable; the **soft-law** Financial Stability Board governs systemic financial risk reasonably well. What separates effective from ineffective regimes is not legal formality but **verification, aligned incentives, financing, and enforcement.** Adding a treaty is not automatically progress; some of the highest-leverage fixes are strengthening verification and finance inside instruments that already exist.

### The governability spectrum (a lens for the dashboard)

- **Tractable** — discrete, technical, shared interest, few key actors: asteroid impact (N1), chemical weapons (T4). Governance can approach a "solved" state, as ozone shows.
- **Hard but structured** — clear hazard, but with winners and losers and powerful holdouts: nuclear war (T1), anthropogenic pandemics (T2), natural pandemics (N4), systemic collapse (G3).
- **Wicked** — diffuse, contested, fast-moving, with the biggest actors least willing to be bound: climate (E1), uncontrolled AI (T3), power concentration (G4), information collapse (G2), Earth-system tipping points (E3), great-power conflict (G1).

The clear pattern: **governance is strongest where it is least needed and weakest where it is most needed.** Closing that inversion is the central challenge the Global Risk Governance Monitor should dramatise.

This inverse relationship between *risk severity* and *governance strength* is the central finding, and the organising insight of the Global Risk Governance Monitor.
