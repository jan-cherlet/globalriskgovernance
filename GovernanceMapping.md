# Multilateral Governance Mapping for Global Catastrophic and Existential Risks

**Project:** Global Risk Governance
**Deliverable 3 of 4:** For each risk in `MasterList.md`, an inventory of the multilateral and global governance mechanisms that exist to assess and/or manage it, an assessment of how adequate each is, and an identification of the governance gaps.
**Date:** 17 July 2026
**Companion file:** `MasterList.md` (risk definitions, taxonomy, interconnections). Risk codes (N1–U1) are shared across both files.

---

## Part A — Methodology

### A.1 What counts as a "multilateral or global governance mechanism"

For this mapping, a mechanism is included if it meets **both**:

1. **Membership is multilateral or global** — it is constituted by three or more states, or by a universal/near-universal international body, or it operates under a global intergovernmental mandate. Purely national instruments (e.g., the US Planetary Defense Coordination Office, or a national AI act) are noted only as context, not counted as global governance. Regional bodies (e.g., the EU, the Council of Europe) are included **only** where they are explicitly designed to be open to, or set a de facto standard for, the wider world.
2. **It performs a governance function on the risk** — it assesses, monitors, regulates, coordinates, finances, or responds to the risk. This spans the full spectrum from binding treaties to standing UN agencies to scientific assessment panels to soft-law declarations and coordination networks.

Private-sector self-governance, industry standards, and NGO advocacy are outside the definition but are flagged where they fill a vacuum left by the absence of public mechanisms (this itself is a finding).

### A.2 The five assessment dimensions

Each risk's governance is assessed against five dimensions — the questions posed for this deliverable. Together they answer not just "does something exist?" but "is it the *right* something, and does it *work*?"

**1. Function — assess vs. manage.** Does the mechanism only *study, monitor, and warn* about the risk (assessment), or does it also *reduce, regulate, or respond to* it (management)? This distinction matters enormously: the world is consistently better at assessing risks than at managing them. Coded as **Assess / Manage / Both**.

**2. Bindingness.** How enforceable is the mechanism? A four-step ladder:
- **Binding + verified** — a treaty with an independent verification/compliance body and consequences (strongest).
- **Binding** — a legally binding treaty, but without real verification or enforcement.
- **Soft law** — political declarations, frameworks, voluntary targets, codes of conduct.
- **Advisory / none** — assessment or dialogue only, or no instrument at all.

**3. Inclusiveness.** Are the actors who matter actually inside the mechanism? A regime can be near-universal yet miss the two or three states that determine the risk (e.g., an arms-control regime that the largest possessors have left). Coded as **Universal / Broad / Club / Narrow**, with a note on which critical actors are absent.

**4. Scope adequacy.** Does the mechanism's mandate cover the whole risk, or only a slice of it? A body may govern one pathway of a risk (e.g., chemical weapons) while an adjacent pathway (biological weapons verification) goes uncovered.

**5. Effectiveness.** Is there evidence the mechanism is actually holding the risk down — a track record, measurable results, or at least credible capability? Legal form is not a proxy for this: some binding treaties are ignored, and some soft-law bodies (e.g., the Financial Stability Board) work well. Coded qualitatively as **Proven / Partial / Weak / Untested**.

### A.3 Overall coverage rating

Synthesising the five dimensions, each risk is given a single **governance-coverage rating** for quick comparison and for the eventual dashboard:

- **Robust** — a binding, broadly inclusive, well-scoped regime with a track record (rare).
- **Developing** — real institutions and instruments exist and function, but with material weaknesses in bindingness, scope, or effectiveness.
- **Weak** — fragmented, mostly soft-law, or ineffective coverage; large parts of the risk ungoverned.
- **Minimal** — little or no dedicated multilateral governance; effectively an orphan risk.

A rating may carry a trend flag (**↓ eroding**, **↑ strengthening**) where the trajectory is significant.

## Part B — Per-risk governance mapping

### Family N — Natural risks

**N1. Asteroid / comet impact — Coverage: Developing**
*Mechanisms:* UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) endorsed, and the UN General Assembly established (2013), two standing bodies: the **International Asteroid Warning Network (IAWN)**, chaired by NASA, which coordinates detection, tracking and characterisation (assessment), and the **Space Mission Planning Advisory Group (SMPAG)**, chaired by ESA, which does mitigation/deflection planning (management planning). Deflection is technically proven (NASA's DART, 2022). National assets (NASA, ESA planetary-defence offices) do the heavy lifting.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both, but weighted to assessment. **Bindingness** Advisory — IAWN/SMPAG recommendations are not enforceable. **Inclusiveness** Broad but incomplete; not all spacefaring or affected states are members. **Scope** Strong on near-Earth asteroid detection/warning; thin on long-period comets (short warning time) and on the *decision* to act. **Effectiveness** Detection proven and recently exercised — the 2024 YR4 scare (briefly a ~1–3% impact probability for 2032) activated IAWN/SMPAG procedures for the first time in earnest.
*Gap:* There is **no binding protocol for who authorises, commands, and funds an actual deflection mission**, nor how the world decides collectively under uncertainty. Detection is governed; the response decision is not.

**N2. Supervolcanic eruption — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* No dedicated global regime. The **UNDRR Sendai Framework** covers disaster risk reduction generically; scientific coordination runs through non-governmental bodies (IAVCEI, the Global Volcano Model); ICAO's Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres address aviation ash only; WHO/OCHA would handle humanitarian response.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess (scientific monitoring) at best. **Bindingness** None. **Inclusiveness** n/a. **Scope** Grossly inadequate to the hazard — nothing addresses the defining threat of a supereruption, namely multi-year global agricultural collapse. **Effectiveness** Untested.
*Gap:* One of the least-governed catastrophic risks. **No mechanism monitors supervolcanoes as a global catastrophic threat, and none prepares for the civilisational consequence — global harvest failure** (e.g., strategic food reserves, resilient-food contingency planning). Unpreventable and near-ungoverned.

**N3. Severe space weather — Coverage: Weak**
*Mechanisms:* The **World Meteorological Organization** coordinates space-weather services; **ICAO** has run global space-weather advisory centres for aviation since 2019; the **International Space Environment Service (ISES)** links national forecasting centres; COPUOS and UNDRR provide fora. Operational forecasting is national (NOAA SWPC and equivalents).
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess (monitoring/forecasting); management is essentially national grid-hardening. **Bindingness** None globally. **Inclusiveness** Broad on the science side. **Scope** Covers forecasting; does not cover resilience standards for the systems at risk (grids, satellites). **Effectiveness** Partial — good short-term forecasting, little mitigation.
*Gap:* **No binding global standards for hardening electricity grids and critical infrastructure** against a Carrington-scale event, and the risk is repeatedly flagged as under-prioritised (UN Global Risk Report). Assessment without management.

**N4. Natural pandemic — Coverage: Developing ↑**
*Mechanisms:* The **World Health Organization** is the hub; the **International Health Regulations (2005)** are legally binding on 196 states (with 2024 amendments strengthening them); the **WHO Pandemic Agreement** was adopted by the World Health Assembly on 20 May 2025 — only WHO's second-ever binding treaty — though it is **not yet in force**: its Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) annex is still being negotiated, with a final outcome now targeted for the World Health Assembly in May 2027. Supporting bodies: **CEPI** (vaccine R&D), **Gavi** (access), and the **WHO Hub for Pandemic and Epidemic Intelligence** (Berlin).
*Assessment:* **Function** Both. **Bindingness** Binding (IHR) but historically weakly enforced; the new Agreement adds binding architecture once ratified. **Inclusiveness** Near-universal — but the 2025 US departure from WHO is a serious hole in the regime. **Scope** Broad (prevention, preparedness, response), though equity and supply-sharing (the crux of PABS) remain unresolved. **Effectiveness** Partial — COVID-19 exposed compliance, speed, and equity failures; reforms are a direct response.
*Gap:* **Compliance and enforcement of the IHR, the unfinished equity/PABS bargain, sustained financing, and the withdrawal of a major power.** Strengthening, but not yet a proven regime.

*(Sub-threshold natural hazards — earthquakes, regional volcanism, floods — are governed, where at all, through UNDRR/Sendai and are outside this catastrophic-risk mapping.)*

### Family T — Anthropogenic-technological risks

**T1. Nuclear war — Coverage: Weak ↓ (eroding)**
*Mechanisms:* The **Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT, 1970)** is near-universal and anchors the regime; the **IAEA** verifies safeguards (assessment/verification); the **Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT)** is not in force but its CTBTO operates a global monitoring network; the **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW, 2021)** is in force but no nuclear-armed state has joined; regional nuclear-weapon-free-zone treaties cover much of the global South. **Bilateral US–Russia arms control has now collapsed: New START expired on 5 February 2026 with no successor and no negotiations underway** — for the first time since 1972 there are no treaty limits on the two largest arsenals. The Conference on Disarmament remains deadlocked.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both (IAEA/CTBTO assess; NPT/arms-control manage). **Bindingness** Binding + verified for non-proliferation (IAEA); **the binding cap on deployed strategic arsenals has just lapsed**. **Inclusiveness** NPT broad, but the disarmament and arms-control layer excludes the actors that matter — China is in no arms-control framework, and the nine possessors sit outside the TPNW. **Scope** Non-proliferation well-covered; **arms limitation and disarmament now largely uncovered**. **Effectiveness** Partial and worsening — proliferation broadly contained, disarmament stalled, and the guard-rails on the US/Russia/China arsenals are weakening.
*Gap:* **The disappearance of binding numerical limits on the largest arsenals, the absence of China from any arms-control regime, and the risk of a renewed three-way arms race.** A regime moving from Developing toward Weak in real time.

**T2. Engineered / synthetic pandemic — Coverage: Weak**
*Mechanisms:* The **Biological Weapons Convention (BWC, 1975)** bans bioweapons and binds 180+ states — but has **no verification organisation or compliance mechanism**, a gap unclosed for 50 years. The **UN Secretary-General's Mechanism** can investigate alleged use. The **Australia Group** coordinates export controls (a narrow club of ~40+ states). WHO/IHR and the new Pandemic Agreement touch biosafety/lab-leak questions.
*Assessment:* **Function** Manage (prohibition) but not verify. **Bindingness** Binding but unverifiable — the defining weakness. **Inclusiveness** BWC broad; Australia Group is a Western-led club that many states view as exclusionary. **Scope** Bans state bioweapons; leaves **dual-use gain-of-function research, lab biosafety, and the AI-enabled design of pathogens largely ungoverned.** **Effectiveness** Weak — no way to detect cheating.
*Gap:* **No BWC verification protocol; no global governance of dual-use biotechnology or of the AI–bio convergence** that is lowering the barrier to engineered pathogens (see interconnection with T3).

**T3. Artificial intelligence — misalignment & loss of control — Coverage: Minimal → Weak ↑**
*Mechanisms:* There is **no binding global treaty on AI safety.** What exists is a fast-emerging patchwork: the **Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (2024)** — the first legally binding international AI treaty, signed by the US, UK, EU and others, but focused on human rights/democracy rather than catastrophic/frontier safety; the **UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** (established by GA resolution August 2025, members appointed February 2026) — a nascent "IPCC for AI" (assessment); the **Global Dialogue on AI Governance** (first convened Geneva, 6–7 July 2026); the **Global Digital Compact** (2024, part of the Pact for the Future); the **OECD AI Principles** and **UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics** (both soft law); and the summit/AI-Safety-Institute network (Bletchley 2023 → Seoul → Paris). The EU AI Act is regional.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assessment and dialogue are forming; management is essentially voluntary. **Bindingness** Almost none for safety — the only binding instrument is rights-focused. **Inclusiveness** The new UN bodies are universal in principle, but capability is extraordinarily concentrated: roughly **75% of global training compute in the US and ~15% in China** — two states hold ~90%. **Scope** No mechanism governs frontier-model development, compute, or loss-of-control risk. **Effectiveness** Untested; the UN panel's own July 2026 finding is that **capabilities are accelerating faster than any government can regulate**, with "no technical guarantee" that advanced systems follow instructions.
*Gap:* The **single largest gap relative to risk magnitude** — for the risk several sources rank as the top long-run existential threat, there is no binding global safety regime, no frontier-model oversight, and no governance of compute concentration. Institutions are appearing (hence ↑) but far behind the technology.

**T4. Military AI / autonomous weapons — Coverage: Weak**
*Mechanisms:* The **Group of Governmental Experts on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (GGE on LAWS)** under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons has deliberated since 2014 but is consensus-bound and slow. A 2023 UN General Assembly resolution on autonomous weapons drew 156 states. The **REAIM** summit process (2023 Hague, 2024 Seoul) produced a political declaration on responsible military AI. Momentum is building: at the March 2026 GGE session, support for opening negotiations on a binding instrument grew to 70+ states, and the **CCW Review Conference in November 2026** could adopt a mandate to negotiate — but **the US and Russia oppose new binding rules.**
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess/deliberate. **Bindingness** Soft law only, so far. **Inclusiveness** Broad debate, but blocked by key militarily-advanced states. **Scope** No coverage of autonomous weapons, meaningful human control, or **AI integration into nuclear command and control** (the escalation danger flagged in GCF 2026). **Effectiveness** Weak — years of talks, no instrument.
*Gap:* **No binding rules on autonomous weapons or on human control over the use of force; the AI–nuclear escalation nexus is entirely ungoverned.** A binding mandate is possible at the November 2026 Review Conference, but opposed by the states that matter most.

**T5. Other WMD (chemical, biological, radiological) — Coverage: Robust (chemical) / Weak (biological) — mixed**
*Mechanisms:* The **Chemical Weapons Convention (1997)** with the **OPCW** is the gold standard: near-universal, legally binding, with intrusive verification and destruction of declared stockpiles (Nobel Peace Prize 2013). Biological weapons fall under the weak BWC (see T2). Radiological security runs through the **IAEA**, the **Convention on the Physical Protection of Nuclear Material** (and its 2005 amendment), the **International Convention for the Suppression of Acts of Nuclear Terrorism**, and the IAEA Code of Conduct on radioactive sources.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both (chemical: verify + manage). **Bindingness** Binding + verified (chemical); binding-unverified (biological); binding (radiological/nuclear-material). **Inclusiveness** Near-universal for CWC. **Scope** Chemical well-covered; biological under-covered; radiological source-security uneven across states. **Effectiveness** Proven for chemical (though Syria and assassinations show enforcement limits when a state defies the regime); weak for biological.
*Gap:* The **biological leg (verification) and uneven radiological-source security.** The chemical regime is a model of what the others could be.

### Family E — Anthropogenic-environmental risks

**E1. Catastrophic climate change — Coverage: Developing (comprehensive but under-performing)**
*Mechanisms:* The **UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (1992)**, the **Paris Agreement (2015)**, and the **IPCC** (a strong, authoritative assessment body) form the most complete architecture of any risk here — universal membership, annual COPs, and finance vehicles (Green Climate Fund, the new loss-and-damage fund). But Paris targets (Nationally Determined Contributions) are **self-set and not legally binding**, and only the *procedures* (reporting, ratcheting) bind.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both — exemplary assessment (IPCC), managing via Paris. **Bindingness** Procedurally binding, substantively voluntary. **Inclusiveness** Near-universal, though repeated US withdrawals undermine it. **Scope** Comprehensive on mitigation/adaptation/finance. **Effectiveness** **Weak-to-partial — the defining problem:** emissions and warming continue to track well above the 1.5 °C pathway despite the machinery.
*Gap:* **No enforcement of national targets, a persistent ambition-and-finance shortfall, and no mechanism to compel the largest emitters.** The rare case where the institutions are comprehensive but the *results* fall short — a management-effectiveness gap, not an architecture gap.

**E2. Ecological collapse & biodiversity loss — Coverage: Weak–Developing**
*Mechanisms:* The **Convention on Biological Diversity (1993)**; the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022)** with 23 targets for 2030 (including "30×30"); **IPBES** (a strong science-policy assessment body, the biodiversity analogue of the IPCC); and sectoral treaties — **CITES** (wildlife trade, genuinely effective in its niche), **Ramsar** (wetlands), **CMS** (migratory species). COP16 (2024–25) agreed a resource-mobilisation strategy targeting US$200 billion/year by 2030.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both, but assessment stronger than management. **Bindingness** Binding framework, **voluntary targets.** **Inclusiveness** Broad — but the **United States is not a party to the CBD**, a major hole. **Scope** Broad on paper. **Effectiveness** Weak — the predecessor Aichi targets were almost entirely missed, and biodiversity finance is far behind schedule.
*Gap:* **Enforcement, finance mobilisation, and the absence of a key state (US).** A history of missed voluntary targets.

**E3. Earth-system tipping points / planetary boundaries — Coverage: Minimal (structural orphan)**
*Mechanisms:* **No governance regime is organised around Earth-system integrity or tipping-point interactions as such.** Individual elements are partially covered by separate silos — climate (UNFCCC), biodiversity (CBD), oceans (**UNCLOS**, and the **BBNJ / High Seas Treaty**, which entered into force on 17 January 2026, closing one long-standing gap over the ~half of the planet that is high seas), ozone (Montreal). But the *interactions* — AMOC collapse, ice-sheet loss, permafrost thaw, the nitrogen/phosphorus cycles — and the concept of staying within a safe operating space have **no institutional home.** "Planetary boundaries" remains a scientific construct (Stockholm Resilience Centre / Earth Commission).
*Assessment:* **Function** Fragmented assessment; essentially no integrated management. **Bindingness** None at the Earth-system level. **Inclusiveness** n/a. **Scope** The scope gap *is* the risk — no mandate matches it. **Effectiveness** n/a.
*Gap:* **The largest structural gap on the map.** GCF 2026's core argument is precisely for a new "planetary commons" / Earth-system-stability governance to fill it. Nothing currently governs the biophysical thresholds that keep Earth habitable as an interacting whole.

**E4. Stratospheric ozone-layer depletion — Coverage: Robust (the model regime)**
*Mechanisms:* The **Vienna Convention (1985)**, the **Montreal Protocol (1987)** — the **only universally ratified treaty in UN history** — and the **Kigali Amendment (2016)** phasing down HFCs. Backed by the **Multilateral Fund** (financing the transition for developing countries) and the TEAP/SAP expert panels (assessment). The ozone layer is on track to recover to 1980 levels around mid-century.
*Assessment:* **Function** Both. **Bindingness** Binding + verified + financed + adjustable (the Protocol can tighten controls without renegotiation). **Inclusiveness** Universal. **Scope** Matches the risk. **Effectiveness** **Proven** — the standout success in global environmental governance.
*Gap:* Minor and residual — illicit CFC-11 emissions detected in the late 2010s, feedstock/exemption loopholes, and emerging pressures (stratospheric solar-geoengineering proposals, rising rocket-launch emissions). Retained on the list as the **positive benchmark**: proof that a catastrophic risk *can* be governed to near-solution when the conditions align.

### Family G — Geopolitical, societal and systemic risks

**G1. Great-power & state-based armed conflict — Coverage: Weak ↓ (eroding)**
*Mechanisms:* The **UN Charter** and **Security Council** (collective security); the **International Court of Justice** and **International Criminal Court** (accountability); UN peacekeeping; and regional organisations (NATO, AU, ASEAN, OSCE). Arms-control regimes (T1) overlap.
*Assessment:* **Function** Manage (collective security). **Bindingness** Charter binding, but enforcement runs through a Security Council **paralysed by the P5 veto.** **Inclusiveness** Universal in membership, but the veto lets any of five states block action, and major powers sit outside the ICC. **Scope** Designed for exactly this risk; **structurally unable to act when a great power is the aggressor.** **Effectiveness** Weak and declining — visible in Ukraine, Gaza, and the broader "retreat of multilateralism" that WEF 2026 frames as an "Age of Competition."
*Gap:* **Security Council reform is blocked; there is no effective check on aggression by (or protected by) a permanent member.** The mechanism exists but is disabled at the moment of need.

**G2. Governance failure & information collapse — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* The **Global Digital Compact (2024)** (part of the Pact for the Future); the UN **Global Principles for Information Integrity (2024)**; UNESCO work on platform governance and media/information literacy; the Human Rights Council on freedom of expression. **No binding mechanism governs disinformation, platform accountability, or the integrity of the information environment.**
*Assessment:* **Function** Soft coordination/assessment. **Bindingness** None. **Inclusiveness** Broad dialogue. **Scope** Minimal versus a transnational, privately-owned information ecosystem. **Effectiveness** Weak/untested; also in genuine tension with free-expression protections.
*Gap:* **Near-total.** The risk the UN Global Risk Report ranks as the top "global vulnerability" (mis/disinformation) has essentially no governance regime — and because this is the *meta-risk* that degrades every other mechanism's ability to function, the gap is doubly consequential.

**G3. Global systemic & infrastructure collapse — Coverage: Weak–Developing (uneven by sector)**
*Mechanisms:* **Financial** systemic risk is comparatively well-governed by soft-but-effective bodies — the **Financial Stability Board**, **IMF**, **BIS/Basel Committee**, and **G20**. **Cyber** governance is nascent: the **UN Convention against Cybercrime** was adopted (December 2024) and opened for signature in Hanoi (October 2025) with 70+ signatories, but **only three ratifications by mid-2026 and not yet in force**, alongside non-binding UN norms of responsible state behaviour (OEWG). Energy, food, water, and supply-chain resilience are handled by fragmented sectoral bodies (IEA, FAO, etc.).
*Assessment:* **Function** Both in finance; mostly assess elsewhere. **Bindingness** Soft law (finance, effective) to nascent-binding (cyber). **Inclusiveness** G20/FSB are clubs; the cyber convention is broad but unratified. **Scope** No mechanism addresses **cascading, cross-sector systemic risk ("polycrisis")** as an integrated whole. **Effectiveness** Partial (finance) to weak (cyber, cross-sector).
*Gap:* **No integrated governance of cascading systemic risk or critical-infrastructure interdependence**; cyber governance is real but not yet in force.

**G4. Extreme concentration of power & wealth — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* Almost none at the global level. Competition/antitrust law is **national.** The closest instruments are tax-related: the **OECD/G20 global minimum corporate tax (15%)** — weakened by a January 2026 "side-by-side" arrangement accommodating US objections — and the **UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation**, now under negotiation (2025–2027, targeted for adoption 2027). **No mechanism governs the concentration of transformative technology (notably AI compute), monopoly power, or the "lock-in" / unrecoverable-dystopia pathway.**
*Assessment:* **Function** Partial assessment (tax transparency). **Bindingness** Soft/negotiating. **Inclusiveness** OECD is a club; the UN tax process is more inclusive but early. **Scope** Addresses tax slivers, not power concentration or technological lock-in. **Effectiveness** Weak/too early.
*Gap:* **Among the least-governed risks** — no global antitrust, no governance of AI-power concentration, and no instrument aimed at the existential lock-in pathway. A largely novel governance frontier.

### Family U — Unknown and emerging risks

**U1. Unknown / unforeseen & emerging risks — Coverage: Minimal**
*Mechanisms:* The **Pact for the Future (2024)** and its **Declaration on Future Generations** commit the UN to strategic foresight and long-term thinking, and a **UN futures/foresight capacity** is being built. But institutionalised, standing horizon-scanning for novel catastrophic risks is nascent and non-binding, and governance of genuinely emerging technologies (e.g., advanced nanotechnology) is fragmentary.
*Assessment:* **Function** Assess (foresight), aspirationally. **Bindingness** None. **Inclusiveness** Universal in principle. **Scope** By nature can never be complete, but current foresight capacity is thin. **Effectiveness** Untested — the system remains largely reactive.
*Gap:* **No standing, well-resourced anticipatory mechanism** to detect and govern risks before they mature; governance remains reactive when the risks that have historically hurt most were the unforeseen ones.

## Part C — Overarching assessment

Seven cross-cutting findings emerge from the mapping. Together they describe a multilateral system that is far better at naming catastrophic risks than at governing them, and that is weakening precisely as the risks intensify.

**1. The system assesses far better than it manages.** For almost every risk, the assessment layer is stronger than the management layer. The world has authoritative scientific bodies — the IPCC, IPBES, the new UN AI panel, IAWN, CTBTO monitoring — but the instruments that would actually *reduce* the risks are weaker, softer, or missing. This mirrors the UN Global Risk Report's own finding that institutions are strongest at *identifying* risk and weakest at *responding* to it. Knowing is not governing.

**2. Genuinely robust regimes are rare, and they share a common recipe.** Only the ozone regime (E4) and the chemical-weapons regime (T5-chemical) qualify as **Robust**, and both combine the same five ingredients: a **binding** treaty, **independent verification**, **near-universal** membership, **financing** for the transition, and the **adjustability** to tighten over time. Where these five co-occur, catastrophic risks have been driven down dramatically. Where any is missing — verification in the BWC, enforcement in Paris, universality in the CBD — effectiveness collapses. This is the template Deliverable 4 should highlight, and the yardstick against which every other regime falls short.

**3. Governance maturity is inversely related to risk velocity and novelty.** The risks that are newest, fastest-moving, and rated most severe for the long term — **AI misalignment (T3), engineered pandemics (T2), Earth-system tipping points (E3), and power concentration (G4)** — are precisely the least governed. The mature regimes govern older, more discrete hazards (chemical weapons, ozone, non-proliferation). The frontier of risk has outrun the frontier of governance, and the UN AI panel's July 2026 warning that "capabilities are accelerating faster than any government can regulate" is the sharpest example.

**4. The multilateral system is eroding at the moment it is most needed.** Several load-bearing mechanisms have weakened in 2025–2026: **New START expired (February 2026) with no successor**, removing binding limits on the two largest nuclear arsenals for the first time in over 50 years; the **Security Council remains veto-paralysed**; and major powers have **withdrawn from key regimes** (US from the WHO and, repeatedly, from the Paris process). This is the G1/G2/G4 meta-risk biting the governance system itself: a "retreat of multilateralism" (WEF 2026's "Age of Competition") that degrades the world's capacity to manage *every* other risk. Governance is not merely lagging — in places it is going backwards.

**5. Several catastrophic risks are effectively orphans, with no institutional home.** **Supervolcanoes (N2), Earth-system tipping points as an interacting whole (E3), extreme power concentration (G4), and unknown/emerging risks (U1)** have little or no dedicated multilateral governance. These are not risks that are governed badly; they are risks that are barely governed at all. The Earth-system gap is the most structurally important, because the risk is planetary and rising, and it is the explicit target of GCF 2026's call for planetary-commons governance.

**6. Governance is siloed while the risks are interconnected.** As `MasterList.md` §7 sets out, the risks amplify one another — AI accelerates bio-risk and disinformation; climate drives conflict and displacement; nuclear war is itself a climate catastrophe. Yet the governance is organised in vertical silos (one treaty per hazard) with **no mechanism that addresses cascades, convergence, or polycrisis.** The AI–bio convergence, the AI–nuclear-command nexus, and the climate–conflict chain each fall between the cracks of separate regimes. The interconnection map has no governance counterpart.

**7. Legal form is not the same as effectiveness.** The mapping cautions against equating "binding" with "working." The Paris Agreement is binding-in-procedure yet under-performs on results; the BWC is binding yet unverifiable; meanwhile the **soft-law** Financial Stability Board governs systemic financial risk reasonably well. What separates effective from ineffective regimes is not legal formality but the presence of **verification, aligned incentives, financing, and enforcement.** This matters for prescriptions: adding a treaty is not automatically progress, and some of the highest-leverage fixes are strengthening verification and finance inside instruments that already exist.

### The governability spectrum (a lens for the dashboard)

The risks sort onto a spectrum of *governability* that the interactive dashboard can make visible:

- **Tractable** — discrete, technical, shared interest, few key actors: asteroid impact (N1) and ozone (E4). Here governance can approach a "solved" state.
- **Hard but structured** — clear hazard, but with winners and losers and powerful holdouts: nuclear war (T1), chemical/bio weapons (T5/T2), pandemics (N4). Regimes exist but strain against national interest.
- **Wicked** — diffuse, contested, fast-moving, with the biggest actors least willing to be bound: climate (E1), AI (T3/T4), power concentration (G4), information collapse (G2), Earth-system tipping points (E3). These are where the gaps are widest and the stakes highest.

The clear pattern: **governance is strongest where it is least needed (tractable, lower-probability risks) and weakest where it is most needed (wicked, high-severity, escalating risks).** Closing that inversion is the central challenge this project's dashboard should dramatise.

**Bottom line:** Of 17 named risks, only **two** (ozone; chemical weapons) have robust governance; a handful are **developing**; and the largest, fastest, and most interconnected risks — AI, engineered bio, Earth-system tipping points, power concentration, information collapse — sit in the **weak-to-minimal** band, at the very moment the multilateral system that would govern them is itself eroding. This inverse relationship between *risk severity* and *governance strength* is the central finding, and the organising insight for Deliverable 4's interactive dashboard.
